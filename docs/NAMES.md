# The names: Datapavan and Visadahyu

Both names come from **Old Persian**, the language of the Achaemenid kings (6th–4th century BCE). It is
known almost only from royal inscriptions carved in stone, written in cuneiform, and usually set beside
Elamite and Babylonian versions of the same text. The vocabulary that survives is small, so every word
below is either attested in those inscriptions or explicitly marked as reconstructed (`*`).

| | Datapavan | Visadahyu |
|---|---|---|
| Role | The product | The foundation (planned) |
| Old Persian parts | *dāta-* "law" + *pāvan-* "protector" | *visa-* "all" + *dahyu-* "land, nation" |
| Meaning | "Protector of the law" | "Of all nations" |
| Pronunciation | DAH-tah-pah-vahn | vee-sah-DAH-hyoo |

---

## Datapavan

### *dāta-*: "law"

- **Meaning.** Law, decree, what has been set down. It comes from the verb root *dā-* "to put, to establish" (Proto-Indo-European *\*dʰeh₁-*): a law is literally "that which has been established".
- **In the inscriptions.** It is attested in Darius I's Behistun inscription (about 520 BCE).
- **Its journey.** The word travelled far beyond Persia:
  - **Hebrew and Aramaic.** It was borrowed during the Persian period as *dāt* "law, decree". In Biblical Hebrew it appears in the Book of Esther and in Ezra 8:36. Biblical Aramaic uses it too, most famously in Daniel 6 for "the law of the Medes and Persians", which could not be changed. In Modern Hebrew, *dat* has come to mean "religion".
  - **Persian.** It lives on as *dād* "justice", in *dādgāh* "court of law", *dādgar* "just" and *dādsetān* "prosecutor".
- **The English coincidence.** To an English reader, *data* means data, and this is deliberate wordplay, not a shared origin. Latin *datum* "a thing given" comes from a different root (*\*deh₃-* "to give"). The coincidence suits the product, which is meant to keep the law where data is concerned.

### *pāvan-*: "protector"

- **Meaning.** Guardian, protector. It comes from the verb root *pā-* "to protect, to watch over" (Proto-Indo-European *\*peh₂-*). The same root gives Sanskrit *pāti* "he protects".
- **In the inscriptions.** It appears in *xšaçapāvan-*, "protector of the realm". This was the title of the Achaemenid provincial governors. The Median form of the same title, *\*xšaθrapāvan-* (Old Persian *ç* corresponds to Median *θr*), gave Greek *satrápēs* and English *satrap*.
- **In today's Persian.** A close relative of the same root lives on in the Persian suffix *-bān* "keeper" (from a reconstructed Old Iranian *\*pāna-*). Persian speakers use it every day: *negahbān* "guard", *darbān* "doorkeeper", *bāghbān* "gardener", *marzbān* "border warden".

### The compound

*Datapavan* = *dāta-* + *pāvan-*, "protector of the law". It is built on the same pattern as
*xšaçapāvan-* ("realm" + "protector"). The compound itself does not occur in the inscriptions; it is
coined from two attested parts.

**What it stands for:** an app that stands guard on the user's behalf and enforces the rules about what
may leave the phone, in the spirit of data-protection law.

## Visadahyu

### *visa-*: "all, every"

- **Meaning.** All, every, whole. Related words: Avestan *vīspa-* and Sanskrit *viśva-* "all".
- **In the inscriptions.** Its clearest Old Persian attestation is *visadahyu-* itself. Some royal titles use the Median form *vispa-* instead, as in *vispazana-* "of all kinds of people".

### *dahyu-*: "land, country, people"

- **Meaning.** Land, country, and also the people who live in it. In the inscriptions, *dahyāva* (plural) are the lands and peoples of the empire, listed one by one.
- **Its journey.** It lives on in Persian as *deh* "village", and in *dehqān*, originally a landed gentleman and later a farmer.

### The compound and its monument

*Visadahyu* means "of all nations". It comes from the inscription of Xerxes I (reigned 486–465 BCE) on
the **Gate of All Nations** at Persepolis (inscription XPa):

> *duvarθim visadahyum* — "this gateway of all nations"

The gate was the formal entrance to the Persepolis terrace, and the reliefs of the city show envoys of
many lands carrying their gifts. Two features of the gate make the name especially fitting:

- **It is multilingual.** Its inscription is written in three languages (Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian), just as Datapavan is built to work in every language.
- **It is guarded.** Colossal bulls flank its western doorway, and *lamassu* (human-headed winged bulls) its eastern one; the trilingual inscription is carved above each of the four figures. A gate open to all nations, with a guardian at the door, is the relationship between the foundation and its product.

**What it stands for:** a foundation that belongs to no single nation or company, open to everyone, and
working for people in all lands.
